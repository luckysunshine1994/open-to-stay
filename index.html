A mixed reality artwork with Belfast City of Sanctuary

Open to Stay

Where do you feel at home, and what keeps that place alive?

↓

Open to Stay is an interactive mixed reality artwork about places of sanctuary. It is made from conversations with people in Belfast who have built a life in more than one country.

When you start again somewhere new, you slowly find small anchor points: a river, a park, a library, your own front door. Places of rest and calm. Sometimes one of these places becomes a doorway to another place, far away, where you once built a life before. What do these places mean to us? How fragile are they? And what can each of us do to protect them, and make more of them?

“As human beings, we are always looking for safe spaces. Spaces we would call sanctuary. Spaces where people feel connected, feel welcome, feel safe.”

Israel, Belfast City of Sanctuary

Learn. Share. Embed. Act.

“What do you learn? You share what you learn, you embed what you have learned, and then you act on it.”

Open to Stay is a mixed reality documentary art piece, designed to be experienced in person, with a headset, in any space: an art venue, a community centre, a classroom or a gazebo in the park. Portals appear around you. Step inside someone’s story and take part in building a sanctuary.

On this page you can listen to the full conversations and read the words of each interview. Tap any line to hear it.

Every day, you see this. Every day, a new colour. Every day there is… look, how the colour… [?]

It really makes my day. It really makes me happy. It makes me look forward to life from a different angle.

You know, tomorrow is going to be better. Tomorrow is going to be something different.

I'm living near the river, and my balcony is facing that place.

Originally I'm from Sudan. I have been here in Belfast for the last ten years.

I didn't come when I was young. I came at this age. Emotionally, my heart and my mind are linked there.

Whenever I'm trying to convince myself that I'm here… still, everything, everything is there.

And whenever I'm on that balcony, it reminds me of my childhood, my family, my mum, my sister.

I've been living in that place for almost one year now. But since I came, whenever I feel that I need to be with myself, I sit on that balcony facing the river.

It gave me courage for life. My husband is [unclear], and it's difficult. But on that balcony, there is hope.

Every day is completely changed: the colour of the water, the lights, the reflection. It gives me courage that tomorrow will be better.

And sometimes, whenever I lose the taste of life because my husband is suffering, I just sit there. I just look into the river.

SudanRiver Gash

And I will show you something. This is a river, and this is a mountain. Look how beautiful it is.

I used to live in the eastern part of Sudan, where there is something called Gash. It's the Gash River.

It is a seasonal one. It's not always there, but in the rainy season it flows heavily, and sometimes it destroys things. But it means a lot to me.

In the east we have mountains, and whenever we wanted to have a good time, we used to go there and sit nearby.

When I was young, we used to go with my family early in the morning to watch the sunrise, you know? And later with my friends, when we were teenagers, the sunset.

Last August, I went home. I went to see my family after the war. I went to that town, because my family are there now, after the war.

As if God was sending me there to heal the injured part of me. Because being away from my family is not easy. I am so attached to my family.

And with the struggle I'm facing here: the language, the culture barrier, many things.

I was living on the Donegall Road, and then they moved us here.

As if it is a message from God: there is something that makes you strong.

Story 3

Home

Kowther

scroll to open

BelfastA safe place

Kowther · Home
0:00

Read along

The place I feel safe is my home. During the hard time, I didn't feel safe outside, but my home became the safe place. I just close the curtains, switch off the light, and have a chat with my family.

During that time, I hosted seventeen of our neighbours. They came to my house and stayed, because they were scared where they were.

So at that moment, I felt my home is safe, not just for me, but for families, for the neighbours, for my community. A place to come and have a chat together.

During the riots we stayed in the house for about one week. I wanted to get fresh air on the balcony, and my mum would say: no, you cannot go out on the balcony. They came and filled bottles with petrol, so they can make bombs.

My mum would say: if you go out during the day, they will see you, and then at night they will come and smash the glass and burn our house.

The first day was good, because we all came together and chatted. Everyone was on their phone, checking on their families in Sudan. They called: are you safe? We said: yes, we are here, we are safe, but we cannot go outside.

The first day we had no problem with food. The milk, the bread, everything was good. By the end of the day we didn't have anything. The kids need milk. There was no sugar. The bread was finished, because there were seventeen of us.

Luckily, one of the organisations we work with, Belfast City of Sanctuary, came to my house. They brought food bags, milk, bread, all the essentials. I could go out and buy it, but because of the situation, I couldn't.

So I think no one should feel that way, scared to leave their house, because some people think you are not welcome here.

It's really scary, because even the police cannot stop them from doing what they are doing. We felt our life did not matter, at that time. That is what really scared me.

They just see you as an immigrant, and they label you like that. Even if I don't have any skills (everyone has a skill, but say I don't have anything), I came to this country because it's not safe back home. So it is my right to feel safe in this country. People should see you as just human.

After the riots, I was scared about the 12th of July. I'm from a Muslim background. Last year, they put a boat on the bonfire, about immigrants, and set fire to it. This year, they put a mosque on top of the bonfire, and they burned it at the end. It's an Islamophobic thing to do.

It's obvious you cannot do this, but some people are okay with this. And so I felt: no, I'm not really safe here, and I don't belong. The only thing I'm expecting for next summer is for it to happen again.

Story 4

The Library

Israel

scroll to open

Northern IrelandLibraries and new sanctuaries

Israel · The Library
0:00

Read along

I've been in Belfast now for twenty years. That's a very long time.

When I first moved here, in 2009, I was very ill. I couldn't go to work. But I couldn't stay at home. I needed to go somewhere, and the library gave me that space.

I would pick a book, read, and leave it there. Come back the next day, pick it up. I didn't take books home, so I always had something to look forward to.

So every day I had my plan for the week. Three days, I would go and read a book and leave it there, hoping nobody would take it. Sometimes I would hide it somewhere, so nobody takes it, so I can come back and continue.

Because I wanted to come back. If I take it home, I won't leave the house. And I wanted to leave the house, to go somewhere I felt safe.

I joined Belfast City of Sanctuary in 2016. That was the first time I went to a meeting.

One of the streams is Schools of Sanctuary, which I was very involved in. Today we have 111 Schools of Sanctuary, including kindergartens. That is the most amazing part of it.

I went to some of those nursery schools, and the children were fantastic. They would hold hands and play together. They didn't see any difference. And that is how a real world should look.

Higher education is another stream: the colleges and the universities. When you see them open their doors, saying to the community: you can use our space. I think that is amazing.

And another stream is Libraries of Sanctuary. In Northern Ireland we only have one: Ormeau Library, on the Ormeau Road.

What that means is: how welcoming the library has been, being a part of the community. The library is part of the community. It's not a resource from the outside. It's a resource for the community, by the community and with the community.

We are hoping that libraries in Northern Ireland will put other libraries forward.

We have seen riots happen in 2024, 2025, and then 2026. And it's going to happen next year. So that is where the 'act' part comes from. We need to be proactive. It's time for action. There is a call for action.

We want to celebrate organisations that have been acting with us, alone or with other organisations. To be formally recognised, and to stand up and say: we are a welcoming organisation. A public declaration that we are a welcoming organisation. Our community is a welcoming community.
